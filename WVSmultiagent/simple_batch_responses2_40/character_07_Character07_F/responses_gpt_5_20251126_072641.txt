When jobs are tight, fairness to me is threefold: fairness to local workers who’ve paid into the system, fairness to migrants who are often doing hard work on thin margins, and fairness to honest employers who follow the rules. The enemy isn’t people; it’s exploitation and opacity.

How I’d balance it:

- Start with wages and standards, not passports:
  - If an employer truly can’t fill a role locally, they should have to pay a premium wage (say, above the local median for that occupation) to hire a migrant worker. That filters out “cheap labor” motives and proves it’s a real shortage.

  - Require good‑faith recruitment at a fair wage, with job postings in places locals actually look (community colleges, union halls, workforce centers).

- Tie admissions to transparent labor demand:
  - Independent board updates shortage lists by region and sector every 6–12 months. No evergreen lists gathering dust.

- Fund upskilling with an employer levy:

- Protect migrant workers so the floor doesn’t collapse:
  - Visa portability (workers can change employers) and whistleblower protections. If people can’t be threatened with deportation for speaking up, wage theft drops for everyone.
  - Clear, accessible grievance channels and real penalties for violators.

- Separate humanitarian from labor:
  - Refugees/asylees are a moral commitment; don’t make them a pressure valve for the job market. Support quick work authorization plus language/civics classes so they can stand on their own feet.

- Use economic “triggers,” not vibes:
  - If local unemployment in a sector spikes above an agreed threshold, pause or tighten new entries in that band until it normalizes—while honoring renewals and rights for people already here.

  - An earned path to legal status for long‑time, law‑abiding undocumented workers who pay taxes and pass background checks. Bring people into the rules so we can actually enforce them.

  - Direct a slice of visa/levy revenue to schools, clinics, transit, and housing in high‑arrival areas. Pressure on rent and classrooms is real; address it up front.

- Radical transparency and simple compliance:
  - Publish admissions numbers, wage floors, and enforcement results. Sunshine builds trust.
  - Make compliance doable for small businesses—clear forms, one portal, real person on the phone—so the good guys aren’t buried in paperwork while bad actors skate.

The principle underneath all this is the same one I learned in my dad’s shop: pay fairly, post the rules where everyone can see them, and hold everyone to the same standards. When we do that, locals aren’t squeezed, migrants aren’t abused, and the employers doing it right aren’t punished for their integrity.

- Economic triggers: Use sector-specific unemployment and vacancy rates to dial admissions up or down, honoring people already here.
- Equal standards: Same labor laws for everyone, visa portability so workers can leave bad bosses, and stiff penalties for wage theft. That protects locals and migrants alike.

- Law enforcement and public health, to separate rumor from reality on safety and disease.

My bottom line: Most positions in this debate carry a piece of the truth. The trick is to pin arguments to verifiable facts and design rules that reward fair play. Strong borders and clear pathways.
