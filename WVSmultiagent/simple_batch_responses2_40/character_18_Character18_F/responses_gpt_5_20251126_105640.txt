J (扮演Character18_F) 回答: (as Character18_F)

I’m not for swinging to either extreme. “Let everyone in” sounds kind, but if you don’t match it with housing, schools, and fair jobs, you just set folks up to struggle and turn neighbors on each other. “Shut the door” sounds tough, but it ignores our values and the fact that a lot of industries—and families—depend on people moving. I’d pick a blended plan with clear standards, flexible numbers, and real support.

What I’d choose:
- A job‑needs lane with a strong floor: Bring workers in where there’s proven need, but tie it to equal pay for equal work, safe conditions, and visas that let folks change employers so nobody gets trapped. Bad jobs shouldn’t be propped up by desperate workers.
- Flexible numerical limits, not fixed caps: Set ranges that adjust by sector and region based on data—unemployment, wages, and vacancy rates—with an independent commission making recommendations and Congress on the hook to say yes or no.
- A firm humanitarian lane: Keep our promise to refugees and people fleeing violence, with quick work permits and support so families aren’t left in limbo.
- Family reunification with less red tape: Long waits break people. Modernize the system so spouses, kids, and parents aren’t separated for years.
- A path for neighbors already here: If you’ve put down roots—worked, paid taxes, raised kids—there should be a fair way to get right, including DACA folks moving to citizenship.
- Community capacity funding that follows people: For every newcomer settled, send automatic dollars to the receiving schools, clinics, and housing so existing residents don’t feel squeezed and newcomers can land well.
- Real enforcement where it matters: Go after wage theft, shady labor brokers, and companies using temps and misclassification to undercut pay. Protect the right to organize for everyone so standards rise together.
- Border management that’s orderly and humane: More legal entry points, faster asylum decisions, safe conditions—so it’s not chaos at the line and people aren’t dying trying to get here.

Why this balance feels fair:
- Same rules, same floor: When wages and safety are enforced across the board, you don’t pit local workers against migrants—you lift both.
- Belonging takes investment: If we welcome people, we have to fund the places they live. That keeps trust on the block and reduces tension.
- Numbers should match reality: Some years we can take more, some years less. Let data guide us, not panic or politics.
- Dignity is non‑negotiable: Families shouldn’t be split, and people fleeing danger deserve a chance to rebuild, with clear timelines and support.

So, my mix is: job‑demand admissions with worker protections, flexible numeric ranges, a solid humanitarian and family track, and a real on‑ramp for long‑time neighbors—paired with strong labor enforcement and local investment. That’s how you keep fairness and social glue at the same table.
