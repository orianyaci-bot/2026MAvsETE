I’m not for the extremes. “Let anyone come” ignores capacity and undercuts buy‑in; “shut the door” hurts our economy and betrays who we say we are. What I’d back is a mix of job‑demand admissions, numerical limits that adjust, and firm humanitarian and family tracks—anchored by enforcement that targets bad employers, not workers.

The core pieces:
- Employment-based visas tied to real demand
  - Require genuine local recruitment at or above prevailing wages before tapping visas.
  - Set sector caps that adjust with unemployment, wage trends, and demographics, reviewed annually by an independent commission.
  - Make visas portable so workers can change employers without losing status—prevents exploitation and protects local wage floors.
  - Pair with funded training/apprenticeships for people already here so employers can’t skip investing in the local pipeline.
- Numerical limits with flex
  - Overall admissions targets set yearly, with emergency cushions for shocks (disasters, wars).
  - Regional allocations where it makes sense—some places have jobs and housing, others are tapped out—plus incentives for employers to locate where communities have capacity.
- Humanitarian and family pathways
  - Maintain a robust refugee program with thorough vetting and solid resettlement support (housing, ESL, job placement) in the first year.
  - Keep family reunification—spouses, minor kids, and a reasonable track for parents—so people can build stable lives.
- Earned regularization for the long‑settled
  - If you’ve been here for years, worked, paid taxes, and kept a clean record, there should be a way to get right with the law. Bring people out of the shadows, which lifts standards for everyone.
- Enforcement that lifts the floor
  - Hit wage theft, misclassification, and illegal hiring hard—real fines, back pay, debarment from government contracts. Make compliance simple and accountability certain.
  - Phase in verified hiring systems with privacy and due‑process protections, so paperwork isn’t used to intimidate whistleblowers.
  - Create a firewall so reporting labor abuses doesn’t trigger deportation—when workers can speak up, standards rise across the board.
- Border and processing that are credible and humane
  - Resource ports of entry, asylum officers, and immigration courts so claims are decided quickly and fairly. Alternatives to detention that work—case management, check‑ins—especially for families.
  - Go after smuggling networks while honoring our asylum obligations.
- Integration and community capacity
  - Direct funding to schools, clinics, transit, and housing in receiving areas—paid in part by employer fees tied to large inflows.
  - Invest in English classes, credential recognition, and childcare so newcomers get to self‑sufficiency fast.
  - Expectation setting: pay taxes, basic English over time, civics—clear milestones for permanent residency and citizenship.

Why this balances fairness and social integration:
- Fair to local workers: jobs first try to clear at decent wages; training ladders are funded; enforcement stops undercutting through exploitation.
- Fair to migrants: clear rules, real protections, and predictable paths beat the chaos that breeds abuse.
- Fair to communities: numbers that reflect capacity, dollars that follow people, and a say in where and how growth happens.
- Fair to employers who play by the rules: no reward for cutting corners; predictability to plan hiring.

Trade‑offs I accept:
- It’s not the fastest or cheapest for employers compared to a free‑for‑all, but it’s more sustainable and keeps wages from racing to the bottom.
- Integration support costs up front, but it pays back in higher earnings and tax contributions over time.
- Caps risk being too tight or too loose in a given year; a data‑driven commission and annual review are the best guardrails we’ve got.

Bottom line, I want a system that says yes when it should, no when it must, and “here’s how” more often than “good luck.” Strong floors, clear lanes, and the resources to make change feel managed—not dumped—are how you keep solidarity intact while honoring both our economic needs and our values.
