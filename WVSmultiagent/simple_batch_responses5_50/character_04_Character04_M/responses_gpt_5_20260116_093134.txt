Me: Finally, when it comes to actual policy choices, there are different approaches: complete openness, allowing anyone who wishes to come; admitting people based on job demand; setting numerical limits; or refusing entry to workers from other countries. I’d go with a hybrid: admit people based on real job demand, inside clear numerical limits, with a steady humanitarian lane. Not “anyone can come,” and not “nobody can,” but ordered and paced so towns can keep up.

Here’s what that looks like to me:

- Annual caps tied to reality: Set national and sector caps each year based on labor data and local capacity—housing, schools, clinics. An independent board publishes the numbers and the reasons, and Congress signs off.

- Demand-driven visas with guardrails: Shortage lists by region and trade. Mandatory local advertising and a real labor test. Prevailing wage at or above the local median so visas don’t become a discount. Visas are portable so workers can leave a bad boss without falling out of status.

- Employer responsibility: If you use visas, you chip in for apprenticeships, English classes, childcare/transport—whatever keeps locals in the pipeline. No free rides.

- Family and roots: Prioritize nuclear family reunification with reasonable caps so wait times aren’t endless. People do better when their immediate family isn’t in limbo.

- Refugees and asylum: Keep a dedicated, realistic quota for refugees, matched with funding for resettlement and schools. At the border, process asylum quickly with due process—approve the real cases, return the rest—so it’s humane and not chaotic.

- Earned legalization: For people who’ve been here for years, clean record, paying taxes—let them get right through background checks, fines, English/civics, and a waiting period. That stabilizes workplaces and neighborhoods.

- One floor for everyone: Same wage, safety, and overtime rules for all workers, with tough enforcement. Phase in mandatory E‑Verify and hit wage theft and illegal hiring hard—fines, lost licenses. Go after bad‑actor employers and criminals, not families.

- Pace with capacity: Don’t flood one town. Tie placements to where jobs and housing exist, and make employers and the feds help fund added strain on local services.

- Measure and adjust: Public dashboards on wages, complaints, job openings. If a sector’s wages dip or safety issues spike, tighten the valve; if shortages persist even with good pay, open it a notch.

Why this mix? It’s fair. Locals get a first, real shot and a ladder up. Migrant workers aren’t exploited or turned into scapegoats. Communities aren’t blindsided. We meet humanitarian obligations without overpromising. And employers don’t get to play people against each other.

Total openness ignores real limits; a flat “no” hurts the economy and our values. A steady, rules‑based system—same standards, clear paths, firm consequences—is what keeps belonging and buy‑in on both sides.
