How it would work:

- Numerical limits: Annual caps by sector and region, tuned to unemployment rates and real shortages. If an area’s schools, clinics, and housing are maxed out, slow the inflow there until capacity catches up. Review and adjust yearly based on public data, not politics.

- Refugees and asylum: Keep that lane open with solid vetting, quick work authorization, and proper resettlement support. Spread arrivals so one town doesn’t carry it all. This has its own cap and shouldn’t get traded away when industries want more workers.
- Family reunification: Prioritize spouses and minor kids. Extended family is limited and paced so communities can handle it. Sponsors show they can support relatives—no dumping costs on the neighborhood.
- Earned legalization for long‑timers: If someone’s been here for years, working on the books where possible, paying taxes, learning English, and with no serious crimes—give them a way to get right: fees, background checks, civics classes, and a probationary period. Why this balances fairness and integration:

- It keeps communities stable by pacing numbers and funding the basics that make neighborhoods work.
- It honors our humanitarian side without letting chaos rule the process.

Trade‑offs are real—prices might tick up, paperwork gets heavier, and we need to invest in housing and schools. But that beats the two bad extremes: open‑ended chaos or a dead‑end shutdown. A well‑lit front door, a locked back door, and a fair set of house rules—that’s the kind of policy I can live with.
