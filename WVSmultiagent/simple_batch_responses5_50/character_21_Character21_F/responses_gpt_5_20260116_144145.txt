J (扮演Character21_F) 回答: (as Character21_F)

I never liked the extremes. “Open the doors to everyone” sounds kind but ignores housing, schools, and wage realities. “Shut it down” punishes families and chokes sectors that already depend on immigrant labor. I’d choose a middle path that’s orderly, humane, and protective of workers.

My mix would be:

- Admit based on real job demand, with proof: sector panels set shortage lists using local data. Visas come with a prevailing-wage floor (no discount labor), safety rules, and visa portability so a worker can change employers without losing status. If a company recruits abroad, it must also fund local training and credential-bridge programs.

- Set numerical limits that flex: annual caps by category (work, family, humanitarian) with room to adjust midyear if shortages spike or capacity changes. Distribute arrivals to places that actually have housing, school seats, and clinic capacity, and send funding to those communities.

- Keep humanitarian doors open and orderly: clear timelines for asylum/refugee cases, case management instead of warehousing, work authorization while cases are processed. Don’t dump people into towns that can’t absorb them; spread responsibility.

- Respect family unity with guardrails: allow reunification with reasonable caps and faster processing, because stable families integrate better and cause less strain on services.

- “First look” for locals on public-funded jobs: for city and state contracts, post openings locally for a set period. Pair that with strong enforcement against wage theft and misclassification so no one—local or migrant—is exploited.

- Invest in integration the way we invest in roads: ESL and civic orientation, credential recognition, apprenticeships, and small-business support. Make information available in multiple languages. These are cheap compared to the costs of confusion and resentment.

Why this feels fair and workable to me:
- It protects the floor for everyone. If you can’t legally pay less or lock a worker to one boss, you cut out the worst abuses that turn groups against each other.
- It grows capacity. Training locals and recognizing foreign skills means we’re not just swapping people; we’re building a stronger workforce.
- It’s honest about limits. Caps and distribution tied to real capacity keep schools and clinics from breaking, which is where panic starts.
- It’s humane without being chaotic. Refugees and families get dignity and due process; communities get support to absorb them.

From my own path—having a Colombian certificate dismissed, retraining, watching elder care and construction rely on immigrant hands—I learned fairness isn’t a slogan. It’s clear rules, equal protections, and real chances to move up. This mix lets us welcome people for the right reasons, protect those already here, and keep our neighborhoods steady.
