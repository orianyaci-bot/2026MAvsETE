I wouldn’t choose the extremes. No to “anyone can come,” and no to “nobody can.” The sweet spot, for me, is a mix of admitting people based on real job demand with numerical limits that can adjust, plus clear paths for refugees and families, and strong rules to keep wages and safety fair.

What I’d favor:
- Job-demand admissions with a fair wage floor: Bring in workers where shortages are proven—caregiving, farm work, food processing, some trades, and health support roles—but only if employers offer decent pay and conditions. If they can’t fill jobs at a fair wage, then we look abroad.
- Adjustable numerical limits by sector and region: Set ceilings that “breathe” with the economy—tighten when unemployment spikes locally, loosen where shortages are persistent. Review quarterly with public data, not politics.

- Local-first efforts and training: Require employers to post jobs locally and contribute to paid training, apprenticeships, and childcare if they use visa programs. Give neighbors a real shot before reaching outside.

- Fast, clean paperwork: Speed up work authorization and status decisions so people aren’t stuck idle. Slow, confusing systems create shadows where exploitation grows.
- Transparency and regular review: Publish data on openings, wages, rents, school capacity, and community impacts. Adjust the knobs openly so folks see the “why.”

Why this balance:
- Complete openness ignores real limits—schools, housing, and jobs don’t stretch overnight.
- A flat refusal throws away needed hands and treats people like problems instead of neighbors.
- Pure numerical caps without tying to jobs can misallocate people and breed resentment.
- Pure job demand without limits can flood one area and undercut standards if nobody’s watching.

This middle path protects wages and safety, fills true gaps, and keeps communities steady. It says: clear rules, fair chances, and dignity for the people who keep this place running—whether they’ve been here five generations or five months.
