The question isn’t *whether* to include, but *how*, and *at what pace*. Fairness is managing that tension without scapegoating, without nostalgia, without pretending trade-offs don’t exist.

Policy Choices: Trade-offs Behind Four Options

Me: Finally, when it comes to actual policy choices, there are different approaches: complete openness, allowing anyone who wishes to come; admitting people based on job demand; setting numerical limits; or refusing entry to workers from other countries. J (扮演Character35_M) 回答: (as Character35_M)

Complete openness? No. Not practical, not fair in the long run. As principled as it sounds, it ignores capacity, infrastructure, and the social fabric. You can’t sustainably absorb unlimited numbers without straining schools, housing, healthcare—especially when domestic investment is already lagging. And worse, it invites exploitation: smugglers, traffickers, employers who benefit from a permanent underclass with no rights. That’s not freedom—that’s chaos.

Refusing entry to all foreign workers? Equally absurd. We’re not self-sufficient. We never have been. From Irish laborers building canals to H-1B engineers coding AI, immigration has always fueled growth. Shutting it down entirely would accelerate economic stagnation, especially as our population ages. He’d call that nostalgic isolationism—emotionally satisfying to some, but economically suicidal.

So between those poles—yes, admit based on job demand, *but* within a system of numerical limits and clear conditions.

His ideal policy? A points-based model, similar to Canada or Australia—structured, transparent, merit-informed—but adapted for American realities.

Points for education, language proficiency, job offers, age (favoring working-age adults), and even ties to community—like family sponsorship, but limited and reciprocal. Not unlimited chain migration. A cousin once removed shouldn’t be the backdoor to mass entry.

Numerical limits—yes, but adjustable. Set a base cap—say 150,000 annually—then allow temporary increases for verified labor shortages in specific sectors: nursing, skilled trades, STEM. Tied to employer verification, wage parity, and no displacement of qualified locals. If a company wants to hire a foreign engineer, fine—but only after proving they tried and failed to fill the role domestically at competitive pay.

And crucially: no dual-track system. Migrant workers must be fully documented, taxed, protected by labor laws. No guest worker programs that trap people in dependency. Temporary visas are okay, but with a path to permanence if they meet integration benchmarks—language, clean record, civic participation.

Refugees? Keep a separate humanitarian channel—25,000–30,000 per year, vetted rigorously, resettled with support: housing, language training, job placement. Not charity—integration investment. Let states and cities opt in, with federal backing. Don’t dump people into communities unprepared.

Border enforcement? Necessary, but smart—not militarized spectacle. Use technology, intelligence, cooperation with origin countries. Deter illegal crossings not with cruelty, but with certainty: if you come unlawfully, you will be processed quickly—either returned or placed in a legal queue. No “catch and release” creating de facto amnesty.

And deportation? Focus on serious offenders, not families who’ve been here a decade working jobs nobody else wants. He wasn’t sentimental—he believed in rule of law—but he also believed proportionality matters. Punishment should fit the offense.

Fairness, to him, meant predictability and reciprocity. If you enter legally, follow the rules, learn the language, pay taxes, respect norms—you earn belonging. Not automatic citizenship, but a clear road toward it.

Social integration? That can’t be left to chance. Fund adult education, civic orientation classes—what rights mean, what responsibilities entail. Not assimilation into some monoculture, but adoption of core civic values: free speech, gender equality, religious tolerance, peaceful dispute resolution.

He’d say: “We’re not asking newcomers to stop being who they are. We’re asking them to become part of *this* project—the one that lets us disagree without violence, compete without corruption, live side by side without fear.”

So the balance? Moderated openness. Merit-guided, need-based, numerically bounded, integration-enforced.

Not open borders. Not closed doors.

A nation that chooses—not just reacts.

That, for Character35_M, was the only way forward that honored both fairness and reality.
