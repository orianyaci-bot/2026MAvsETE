When jobs feel scarce, fairness starts with two guardrails: don’t pit struggling neighbors against one another, and don’t let employers win by lowering the floor. People feel the game is rigged when work gets cheaper by making workers more disposable. So I’d balance opportunity with standards:

- Equal pay for equal work. Migrant workers should be paid at least the local median wage for the occupation (including benefits). If a job truly can’t be filled locally, it should clear a wage premium, not a discount. That removes the incentive to hire just to cut costs.

- Simple, transparent labor test. Require employers to advertise locally and document outreach, but focus the test on wages and conditions rather than paperwork that can be gamed. - Job portability for visa holders. No tied‑to‑one‑employer visas. If people can change jobs within the same occupation, they’re harder to exploit and less likely to undercut standards, because they can walk away from bad actors.

- Counter‑cyclical admissions. Tie some work‑visa volumes to labor‑market indicators (unemployment, vacancy rates). In downturns, slow categories most directly substitutable with idle local labor and ramp investment in training; keep humanitarian pathways and close family reunification on their own track.

- Employer skin in the game. Charge meaningful visa fees and dedicate the revenue to apprenticeships, community colleges, and re‑skilling for local workers in the same sectors, plus ESL and integration services for newcomers. - Strong enforcement against bad actors. Make wage theft, misclassification, and labor‑broker abuses costly. Joint liability up the contracting chain; whistleblower protections regardless of status; swift debarment from hiring programs for violators. Enforce standards, not stereotypes.

- Support receiving communities. If a town absorbs new workers, help it expand housing, schools, and clinics. A per‑capita federal grant that follows newcomers reduces local resentment by keeping services stable.

- Distinguish programs by purpose. Refugee and asylum systems are about protection, not labor gaps. Family reunification supports stability. Economic streams should be calibrated to genuine shortages and wage floors.

- Path out of the shadow economy. Long‑settled, law‑abiding undocumented neighbors should have an earned legalization route. A permanent underclass is unfair to them and corrosive to labor standards for everyone.

- Sector specifics. In agriculture, eldercare, and some trades, pair modernization (mechanization, productivity, better scheduling) with visas that have portability, housing standards, and real paths to permanence if people put down roots. Don’t build “perma‑temp” systems.

- Worker voice. Make it legal and safe for all workers to organize and report abuses. When the lowest‑paid can bargain, the floor holds for everyone—locals and migrants alike.

- Data and sunlight. Publish occupational shortage lists, wage thresholds, and visa usage in plain language. People accept hard tradeoffs better when they can see how decisions are made.

This approach reflects how I think about fairness: raise the floor, widen the on‑ramps, and be honest about tradeoffs. Local residents should see investment in their skills and neighborhoods, not just new competition. Migrant workers should see dignity and mobility, not dependency on one boss. And employers should compete on quality and innovation, not on who can squeeze labor the hardest.

I’m skeptical of both extremes—total closure treats people as threats, and total openness without standards invites a race to the bottom. A rules‑based, adjustable system, with real enforcement and real investment, gives us a way to share the work and the gains even when times are tight.
