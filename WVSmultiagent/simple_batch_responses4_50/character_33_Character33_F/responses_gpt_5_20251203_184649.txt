1) Raise and enforce labor standards across the board.
2) Invest in local people so they can actually take available jobs.
3) Let migrants fill genuine gaps—with rights, mobility, and real wages—so no one is a shadow worker.

The mix I’d favor:

- A worker rights floor that applies to everyone from day one
  - Same job, same pay, same safety standards—no matter your status or origin.
  - Portable visas so people can change employers without losing their status. That stops bosses from trapping workers and undercutting wages.
  - Real enforcement against wage theft and abusive labor brokers, with joint liability up the subcontracting chain so bad actors can’t hide.

- A job‑demand track, but with guardrails
  - Tie admissions to transparent indicators: persistent vacancies, rising wages that still don’t fill roles, aging‑care needs, seasonal harvests.
  - Require employers to recruit locally at fair wages and hours first, and to fund paid training/apprenticeships for residents.
  - Charge an impact fee that goes to housing, transit, and schools in the communities where hiring happens, so neighbors aren’t left holding the bag.
  - No “guest worker” dead‑ends—if you’re filling a long‑term need and following the rules, you earn a path to permanence.

- Keep humanitarian and family channels strong and separate from labor demand
  - Fast, fair asylum decisions with work permits issued quickly so families can support themselves.
  - A steady, simpler family reunification pipeline—belonging isn’t just about jobs, it’s about keeping households whole.
  - Communities that receive newcomers get funding automatically for language access, clinics, and schools.

- Flexible numerical limits tied to capacity, not vibes
  - Set ranges that adjust with unemployment, vacancy rates, and housing supply, with emergency room for crises.
  - Allocate regionally where there’s demand and infrastructure, instead of funneling everyone into a few overburdened cities.
  - Exempt immediate family and bona fide humanitarian cases from hard caps.

- Integration as a core part of policy
  - ESL with childcare, credential recognition for foreign training, and portable licenses so skills aren’t wasted.
  - Invest in building more housing and enforcing tenant protections—pressure there is what turns neighbors against each other.
  - Support civic participation: simple pathways to residency and citizenship, and multilingual services so people can plug in.

- Orderly, humane border and processing
  - More capacity at ports of entry, better case management, and due process. Focus enforcement on traffickers and exploitative employers, not on families trying to survive.
  - Clear rules so people know their options and timelines—chaos is what fuels fear.

Why this balances fairness and social integration:
- It protects local workers from being undercut by desperation and rewards employers who do right by their workforce.
- It acknowledges real shortages (like elder care and seasonal agriculture) while fixing the conditions—pay, schedules, training—that make “shortages” look worse than they are.
- It treats migrants as neighbors, not a shadow labor pool—rights, mobility, and a future they can plan for.
- It gives communities the resources to absorb change without burning out, which is how belonging grows instead of backlash.

I’m working class; I’ve watched how quickly bosses pit people against each other when the floor is low. Raise the floor, match arrivals to real need and capacity, and fund the basics where people actually live. That’s the combination that feels fair and keeps the garden growing without pulling up anyone’s roots.
