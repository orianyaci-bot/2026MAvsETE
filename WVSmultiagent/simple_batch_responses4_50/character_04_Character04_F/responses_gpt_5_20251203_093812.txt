From Community to Employment: Balancing Considerations When Resources Are Limited

Me: Let's broaden the topic further. When societal resources are limited, particularly when job opportunities are scarce, what constitutes fairness in your view? As Character04_F, how would you seek a balance between local residents and migrant workers?

J (扮演Character04_F) 回答: (as Character04_F)

When jobs are tight, fairness is about sharing both the effort and the protection. I don’t accept the idea that neighbours must be set against each other; the right balance is built from clear rules, equal floors, and honest evidence about where real shortages exist.

How I’d seek that balance:
- Start with the job, not the passport. Improve wages, conditions, and hours so locals can realistically take the roles. If decent offers still go unfilled, that’s a genuine shortage—then targeted migration makes sense.
- Use independent, fast‑updating evidence to define shortages. Publish the data, consult unions and employers, and review quarterly so policy can breathe with the economy.
- Make employer obligations visible. If a firm relies on migrant labour in shortage roles, they should pay into training and apprenticeship funds for locals, partner with TAFEs and universities, and show their domestic recruitment efforts. Shortcuts shouldn’t be cheaper than doing the right thing.
- Equal treatment as the non‑negotiable floor. Same pay for the same job, full workplace rights, strong enforcement against wage theft, and visa rules that allow job mobility so migrants aren’t trapped with a bad employer.
- Pace intake to community capacity. Align numbers with housing, transport, and services, and invest to expand capacity so newcomers don’t intensify bottlenecks. Fairness to locals includes not overwhelming infrastructure; fairness to migrants includes not dropping them into scarcity.
- Keep humanitarian commitments ring‑fenced. Economic cycles shouldn’t decide whether a person fleeing danger finds safety. That stream is a moral duty, managed separately with proper support.
- Plan for downturns. If unemployment spikes, dial back purely economic intakes temporarily, scale up retraining and wage‑support for residents, and protect migrants already here from destitution—allow transitions to other roles, short‑term support, and clear pathways rather than sudden exclusion.
- Be ethical in sourcing. Don’t strip health workers from countries in crisis; use agreements that invest in training pipelines at home and in partner countries.
- Match people to places. Encourage settlement where jobs exist and communities can welcome, with incentives not coercion. Support language, credential recognition, and bridging programs so skills aren’t wasted.

Concrete example: aged care. Lift pay and staffing ratios, fund local training places with paid practicums, improve scheduling to make the work sustainable. If roles still go unfilled, recruit internationally with permanent pathways, robust workplace protections, and a contribution from employers to local training. That serves residents, local jobseekers, and migrant workers together.

Underneath all of this is a simple compact: rights and obligations travel together. Locals should see fair competition and real investment in their skills; migrants should see dignity, equal treatment, and a path to belong. If we hold that line—clear guardrails, practical handrails—most of the tension can be managed without turning neighbours into rivals.

Contingencies:
