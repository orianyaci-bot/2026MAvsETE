I’d go with a middle path: admit people based on real job demand, set clear numerical limits that flex with conditions, and keep a firm humanitarian lane. Not wide‑open, not slammed shut—orderly, fair, and paced so communities can keep up.

What that looks like in practice:

- Labor‑market track (job demand)
  - Real try‑local first: Post jobs locally at a decent wage and benefits for a set period. If no takers, then recruit from outside.
  - Equal pay, strong floor: Same wages and protections for the same job, no matter where you’re from. Benchmarked “prevailing wage” so it’s about skills, not cheap labor.
  - Visa portability: Let workers change employers so bad bosses can’t hold people hostage. That keeps standards up for everyone.
  - Training obligations: If a company hires from abroad, they also fund apprenticeships or certificates at the community college. Grow talent here while filling gaps now.
  - Tight enforcement: Real penalties for wage theft, misclassification, and fake job postings. Protect whistleblowers.

- Numerical limits with smart flex
  - Annual caps by sector, with a mid‑year adjustment band. Tie it to data: vacancy rates, unemployment in that trade, wage trends, and local housing/school capacity.
  - Publish the dashboard so people can see why we’re opening or tightening a spigot.
  - Emergency valves for disasters or critical shortages, with sunset dates.

- Humanitarian lane
  - A set refugee allotment each year, plus surge capacity for crises, with strong vetting.
  - Faster asylum decisions (more judges/caseworkers), work authorization while cases are pending, and funding that follows people to the towns taking them in.

- Family reunification
  - Prioritize spouses, minor kids, and dependent parents. Clear timelines and support for English/civics so folks can plug in faster.
  - Sponsors take some financial responsibility so local services aren’t blindsided.

- People already here long‑term
  - An earned way to get right: background checks, back taxes, fees, basic English/civics, and a probationary period. Bring people out of the shadows so they can’t be exploited to undercut wages.
  - Pair it with strict employer enforcement going forward so we don’t repeat the cycle.

- Community pacing and support
  - Spread arrivals so one school district or housing market doesn’t get crushed.
  - A “community impact fund” from visa fees to help with classrooms, clinics, transit, and affordable housing where numbers rise.

- Border and process
  - Orderly entry points, humane treatment, quick processing, and clear consequences for repeat illegal entry. More tech and staffing to keep lines moving and cartels out.

Why not “anyone who wishes to come”? Because capacity matters—schools, rents, clinics—and fairness to people who’ve been waiting in line matters too. Why not “refuse entry to workers from other countries”? Because some sectors genuinely need help, and we do have a duty to people fleeing real danger. Plus, hard bans just drive things underground and hurt wages more.

This mix feels fair to me: locals get the first shot and better training, workers—wherever they’re from—aren’t used to drive pay down, and communities get the support to keep belonging intact. Most people can live with immigration when the rules are clear, the numbers match what towns can handle, and the benefits and costs aren’t dumped on the same few neighborhoods.
