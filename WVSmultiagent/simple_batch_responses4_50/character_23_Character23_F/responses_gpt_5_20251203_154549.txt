I wouldn’t pick “anyone can come,” and I wouldn’t slam the door shut either. What I’d support
- Annual caps with a dial: Set a national number each year, with room to turn it up or down based on what’s real—job openings, housing vacancies, school capacity. Announce it up front so families and employers can plan.
- Job‑demand visas on equal terms: Sector visas where there’s a true shortage—care work, farm seasons, construction, some trades—only after open local recruiting at fair pay. Same wage for the same job, full safety rules, and the right to switch employers so nobody’s trapped and underpaid.
- Local first on public work: For taxpayer‑funded projects, locals who meet the posted requirements get first crack, plus apprenticeships that bring residents in the door.

- Family reunification with guardrails: Prioritize spouses and minor kids; keep broader categories but within the overall cap so we don’t outrun housing and schools.
- Earned legalization for long‑time neighbors: Folks who’ve lived here for years, worked, paid taxes where possible, and kept a clean record should have a real, step‑by‑step path to legal status—background checks, fees, English/civics, back taxes. Same goes for DACA kids—they didn’t choose this.
- Real workplace enforcement: Mandate a simple, phased‑in verification system with teeth, hit wage theft and misclassification hard, and hold big contractors responsible for their subs. Focus deportation resources on serious criminals and traffickers, not random sweeps of workers.
- Community impact funds: Charge modest fees on shortage visas and set aside money for the towns taking in new workers—schools, clinics, transit, and housing. Spread placements so one district doesn’t carry the whole load.
- Training our own: Tie employer use of shortage visas to investing in apprenticeships and community‑college programs so local kids and mid‑career folks can move into those jobs.
- Transparency: Publish the data—wages, vacancies, violations, class sizes, rents—so the public can see why the numbers are set where they are, and adjust if things get tight.

Why this mix
- Fairness to workers: Same pay and protections for everyone keeps bad actors from undercutting wages and pitting people against each other.
- Predictability for towns: Caps and planning mean schools and clinics aren’t swamped, and neighbors don’t feel blindsided.
- Honesty about the labor market: Some jobs do have real shortages; others are “short” because the pay or schedule stinks. This setup forces employers to raise standards first, then use visas if the gap remains.
- Respect for the law and for people: Secure border and workplace, humane process for refugees, and a way to bring long‑time families out of the shadows so no one’s living under the table.

I’m against complete openness—our housing, schools, and hospitals aren’t bottomless—and I’m against a blanket ban, which would hurt farms, nursing homes, and job sites and goes against who we say we are. A policy that’s job‑demand plus numerical limits, with an orderly humanitarian lane and strong worker protections, is the balance that feels fair and doable to me.
