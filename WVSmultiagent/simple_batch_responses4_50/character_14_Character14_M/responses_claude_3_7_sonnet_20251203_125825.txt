I favor a carefully calibrated combination of merit-based admission and numerical limits, with some flexibility built in. Here's how I'd structure it:

Third, I'd maintain reasonable numerical limits based on our economic needs and integration capacity. These could adjust somewhat based on economic conditions but would provide predictability. Perhaps 500,000 to 750,000 legal immigrants annually - substantial numbers, but manageable.

Fourth, I'd include a modest humanitarian component for genuine refugees facing persecution, perhaps 10-15% of the total. But the emphasis would be on temporary protection until conditions improve in their home countries, with permanent residence reserved for cases where return is impossible.

Some basic knowledge of American history and civic principles would be required for permanent residence. This isn't about erasing cultural identity - it's about ensuring new Americans can fully participate in and contribute to society.

For temporary workers in agriculture and other sectors with genuine shortages, I'd support expanded guest worker programs with strict oversight to prevent exploitation and ensure they don't undercut American wages.

What makes this fair, in my view, is that it's transparent, merit-based, and aligned with national interests while still being generous by global standards. It rewards those who follow the rules rather than those who circumvent them. It acknowledges that immigration is a privilege, not a right, while still honoring our heritage as a nation of immigrants.

For social integration, this approach would bring in people with the tools to succeed and contribute, reducing strain on communities and fostering positive perceptions of immigrants as assets rather than burdens. That's better for everyone - including the immigrants themselves.
