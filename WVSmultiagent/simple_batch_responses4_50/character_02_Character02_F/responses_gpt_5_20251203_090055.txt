When jobs feel tight, fairness to me means three things at once: same floor, same chance, same share of the load.

- Stabilize the workforce we already have. People who’ve been here for years, paying taxes and raising kids, need a real path to status. Keeping them in the shadows lets bad employers undercut wages and makes job sites unsafe because folks are afraid to report. Stability helps both fairness and safety.

- Back up growth with services. If a meatpacking plant hires a hundred new workers, the city and state should kick in “impact funds” for added ESL classes, school aides, and clinic hours. - Keep the data honest and public. Post how many jobs were filled locally, how many after broader recruiting, what wages were offered, and where violations were found. When people can see the process, they’re less likely to assume favoritism.

I won’t pretend there isn’t tension. When my brother’s hours got cut at the warehouse, it was easier to glare at the new crew than to stare down the supervisor who played favorites and shaved timecards. But most of the squeeze I’ve seen comes from choices at the top: temp agencies cycling people to dodge benefits, companies refusing to train, housing costs exploding while wages crawl.

So fairness, to me, is not pitting neighbors against each other. It’s setting one set of rules and enforcing them—wages, safety, hiring practices—and investing so the pie actually grows. Let locals get the first, fair shot. When there’s still a gap, welcome workers in a way that doesn’t turn them into leverage against the people already here. And make sure the folks cashing the checks help pay for the classrooms and clinics that keep the community whole.
