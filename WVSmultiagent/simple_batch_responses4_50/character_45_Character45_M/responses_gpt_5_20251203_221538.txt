J (扮演Character45_M) 回答: (as Character45_M)

I’m not for “anyone can come” and I’m not for “nobody in.” Both ignore reality. I’d back a calibrated mix: admit people based on verified job demand within clear numerical bands, keep a separate, protected lane for humanitarian cases, and maintain a modest, well‑defined family reunification track. The numbers should flex with evidence—labor data and local capacity—not whiplash with politics.

Here’s what that looks like to me:

- Economic channel, tied to real shortages
  - Shortage lists set by an independent commission using public data, updated regularly by region and sector.
  - Employers must recruit locally first and offer the prevailing wage or better.
  - Visas are portable—workers can change employers in the same field—so nobody’s trapped and standards don’t get undercut.
  - Employers pay a skills fee that funds apprenticeships and upskilling for local residents in the same region/industry.

- Numerical limits with automatic stabilizers
  - Each channel has a floor and a ceiling for the year.
  - Bands flex with triggers like unemployment rates, housing vacancy, and school/clinic capacity. If capacity is tight, intake tapers unless funding ramps up to expand it.

- Humanitarian lane, insulated from the job market
  - Refugees and asylees processed on protection criteria, not employer demand.
  - Fast, fair processing; early casework; community sponsorship; clear path to work and stability.

- Family reunification, clearer and quicker
  - Prioritize nuclear family with transparent caps and timelines so people aren’t stuck in limbo for years.

- Equal standards, real enforcement
  - Same labor laws and safety rules for everyone; strong penalties for wage theft and illegal hiring.
  - Identity and work authorization are verified consistently, but the hammer falls on abusive employers, not workers trying to comply.

- Integration as part of the deal
  - Language classes, credential recognition, and civic orientation funded by a mix of employer fees and federal support.
  - Municipal “impact grants” tied to arrivals for housing, schools, and clinics so neighborhoods aren’t asked to absorb costs without help.

- Seasonal and circular options that don’t invite abuse
  - True seasonal visas with predictable re‑entry for fields like agriculture, plus on‑site housing standards and enforcement.
  - For long‑term contributors who put down roots, a straightforward path to permanent residency/citizenship.

- Order at the border and in the queue
  - More legal pathways and efficient processing to reduce irregular flows; smart, risk‑based screening rather than theater.

Why not the extremes? Total openness ignores housing, schools, and wage floors—you end up with chaos and a two‑tier system, which is the opposite of fairness. A blanket refusal ignores real labor gaps and our humanitarian obligations; it also hurts small businesses and families. A demand‑based system within numerical limits, with capacity checks and strong worker protections, makes the rules real and keeps the ladder sturdy for both locals and newcomers.

Fairness, to me, is everyone playing by the same rules and everyone pulling their weight. Social integration happens when we pair clear expectations with tangible support: people get a fair shot to participate, and communities get the resources to welcome without being stretched to breaking. This setup aims at both.
