How I’d balance it, plain and practical:

- Support the places carrying the load: If a town takes in a lot of new workers, send matching funds for schools, clinics, transit, and housing. Strain on services is real; the fix is resources, not finger-pointing.

- Keep the system orderly and humane: Clear, timely processing so people aren’t stuck in limbo, and targeted enforcement against traffickers and shady labor brokers. Chaos helps the worst actors drive wages down.

J (扮演Character31_F) 回答: (as Character31_F)

I don’t like the extremes. “Anyone can come anytime” isn’t fair to the towns that have to absorb the change without resources; “no one can come” ignores reality and shuts the door on people we need and people who need us. I’d choose a middle path: guided openness with hard guardrails.

What I’d back is a mix:

- Admit based on real job demand, with proof. Employers should have to post locally at a fair wage and decent conditions for a set period. If they still can’t fill it, fine—sponsor workers. Tie these visas to prevailing wages, safety standards, and benefits, same for everyone.

- Set numerical limits that flex with data. Caps by sector and region that adjust automatically based on unemployment, vacancy duration, wage growth, and housing/school capacity. Not set-and-forget, not political whiplash—predictable adjustments.

- Keep humanitarian routes strong and planned. Take refugees and asylum seekers with good screening and quick work authorization. Place people where capacity exists and send matching funds to those communities for schools, clinics, housing, and transit.

- Protect family reunification within reasonable caps. Immediate family should move through without decade-long backlogs; it stabilizes households and reduces exploitation.

- Offer earned regularization for long-term neighbors. If someone’s been here working, paying taxes, raising kids, give a path to get right. Stability keeps wages from being undercut.

Guardrails that make the balance work:

- Same work, same pay, same protections. No misclassification to dodge overtime, no sub-minimum backdoor. Enforce labor standards with teeth; hold lead firms liable for violations in their subcontracting chains.

- Portability of status. Let sponsored workers change employers without losing their visa. If people can leave bad jobs, it’s harder to use them to drag standards down.

- Right to organize and to report abuse. Protect all workers—local or migrant—who join a union or file complaints from retaliation.

- Local-first and training. Require a genuine local recruitment window and make employers investing in visas also pay into apprenticeships, community college programs, childcare for shift workers—so residents can train into those roles.

- Orderly, humane processing. Clear timelines, fewer backlogs, target smugglers and exploitative labor brokers, not families trying to navigate the system.

- Community capacity funding. Per-capita grants that follow people to pay for classrooms, clinics, buses, and affordable housing. Strain is real; resources should match it.

Why this feels fair to me:

- It stops the race to the bottom. If no one can be paid less or worked less safely, bosses can’t pit us against each other.

- It’s predictable. Workers and towns can plan; employers can’t game chaos.

- It’s humane without being naive. We meet our obligations to people fleeing danger, and we help them stand on their own fast.

- It keeps families stable and cuts exploitation. People with status aren’t as easy to squeeze.

- It respects places. Growth goes where capacity exists, and help follows the load.

I’ve watched tight labor markets and lean years. What hurts most isn’t newcomers; it’s loopholes and sudden change dumped on communities without support. A demand-based system with flexible caps, firm standards, and real integration funding gives locals a fair shot and newcomers a fair path. That’s the balance I can stand behind: welcome people, keep the floor high, and build the capacity so the neighborhood doesn’t break under the weight.
