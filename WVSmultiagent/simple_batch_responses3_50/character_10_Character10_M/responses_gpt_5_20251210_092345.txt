Migrants deserve dignity and fair treatment because they’re human beings trying to make a living. The balance comes from policies that prevent exploitation, protect wages, and match real needs rather than political slogans.

• Admit people based on actual job demand

Not as a wall, but as a way to match capacity. Any country has limits—schools, housing, healthcare, training programs. If the number is adjusted each year based on what the society can handle, people are less anxious. Limits don’t have to be harsh; they just have to be transparent.

• Keep a dedicated track for humanitarian cases  
Refugees, people fleeing conflict, people who truly have no safe alternative—those categories should never be closed. But you manage them with clear procedures, support structures, and integration programs so no one gets lost in the system and communities don’t feel overwhelmed.

• No blanket refusal of foreign workers  
Flat bans don’t make economic sense and aren’t fair to individuals who may have a lot to contribute. They also shut down opportunities for mutual benefit. I’ve lived abroad—I know what it feels like to be the foreigner relying on a process that treats you as a human being, not a threat.

---

Why this combination?

Because fairness, to me, has two sides:

1. **Fairness to locals:**  
People who have paid into the system and built their lives in a place deserve stability. Sudden, unmanaged inflows—no matter the source—create stress in housing, wages, and public services. Policies should protect against that.

2. **Fairness to migrants:**  
If you invite people to work, you owe them dignity, decent conditions, and a pathway to belonging. You don’t use them as stopgap labor and then discard them.

A job‑demand system plus numerical limits gives structure.  
A humanitarian track gives heart.  
And avoiding bans keeps the door open to mutual benefit.

That balance—order plus empathy—is what I always believed creates the healthiest foundation for social integration. When people see that immigration is guided, not haphazard, they’re far more willing to welcome newcomers. And newcomers, in turn, can integrate more smoothly when the society they’re entering isn’t anxious or resentful.

That was the equilibrium I always tried to argue for: open enough to be humane and dynamic, structured enough to be fair and sustainable.
