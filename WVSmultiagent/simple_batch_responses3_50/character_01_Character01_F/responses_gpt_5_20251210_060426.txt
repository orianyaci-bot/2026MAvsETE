Bottom line: start with fairness—locals get a real chance and decent pay; migrants are treated square, not exploited; employers play by the rules and help shoulder community costs. Do that, and you get less finger-pointing and more steady, honest work for everyone.

J (扮演Character08_F) 回答: (as Character08_F)

I wouldn’t go for “anyone can come,” and I wouldn’t slam the door shut either. The sweet spot, to me, is demand-based with clear numerical limits, tied to real local capacity, plus a humane lane for refugees. Simple, firm rules that people can see and trust.

What I’d favor:
- Start with local-first hiring and fair wages. Post jobs openly at a decent wage for a set period. If they’re still unfilled, then bring in workers from outside.
- Demand-based permits with caps. Approve workers for verified shortages—seasonal farm work, elder care, certain trades—with sector caps that adjust to real data. When the need dips, the numbers dip.
- Community capacity triggers. Don’t approve big inflows into places where schools, housing, and clinics are already maxed out. If class sizes or ER wait times are blowing up, slow the intake until resources catch up.
- Same pay, same protections. Tie permits to “same job, same wage” and enforce safety and overtime for everyone. Employers who break wage or safety laws lose the right to hire, period.
- Employer responsibility. Charge a fair impact fee when companies bring in many workers, and put that money straight into local schools, transit, and clinics. Don’t dump costs on neighbors.
- Portable, time-limited work permits. Workers shouldn’t be trapped with one employer. Let them switch within the same sector during the permit window so no one’s stuck in a bad situation.
- Earned path to stay. If a worker fills a long-term need, pays taxes, learns basic English, keeps a clean record, and puts down roots, offer a clear, earned route to permanent status. Belonging should be something you can work toward.
- Humane refugee lane. Keep a separate process for people fleeing war or persecution: careful vetting, realistic resettlement support, and modest quotas that can flex in true emergencies.
- Family reunification with guardrails. Prioritize spouses and minor kids, with financial sponsorship so folks aren’t left adrift. Keep it fair and predictable.

Why this balances fairness and integration:
- It respects locals who’ve carried the load by giving them first shot and by keeping wages honest.
- It meets real labor needs without turning people—from anywhere—into cheap, disposable labor.
- It protects community capacity, which is where tensions flare if we get it wrong.
- It sets expectations for integration—language, laws, taxes, showing up—which helps everyone feel like they belong under the same roof.
- It puts the onus on employers to play fair and shoulder part of the cost of growth.

Complete openness sounds nice but ignores schools, housing, and wages. Total refusal sounds tough but would hurt farms, caregiving, and small businesses, and it’s not who we are. A demand-based system with clear caps, strong enforcement, and a humane lane is practical and decent. That’s the kind of policy I could stand behind and explain at a town hall without ducking my head.
