*“Fairness starts with honesty about limits.”*

When jobs are tight and budgets stretched, fairness means recognizing that a community has obligations to the people who are already part of its social fabric—taxpayers, long‑time residents, folks who have built the local economy. That doesn’t mean shutting the door on newcomers; it means being deliberate about how many people the system can absorb without undercutting stability for everyone.

A few principles he held:

• **Local workers should have a clear first shot at available jobs.**  
Not as a matter of exclusion, but because the community has an existing commitment to them—schools, roads, pensions, public services funded over decades.

• **Migrant workers fill gaps where locals *can’t* or *won’t* meet demand.**  
He always said: don’t pretend we don’t rely on certain types of labor. The key is matching immigration to real economic need, not to short‑term political whims.

• **Wages shouldn’t be driven down by desperation on anyone’s part.**  
He believed employers sometimes misuse a surplus of willing workers to keep wages artificially low. Fairness means maintaining decent labor standards for all workers—migrant or local—so nobody becomes a tool to undercut anyone else.

• **Integration capacity matters.**  
He wasn’t talking about culture wars—he meant concrete things: housing availability, school capacity, healthcare access. If those systems are already strained, adding more people without support makes things harder for both newcomers and long‑timers.

How he’d balance it:

1. **Set job‑based entry tied to verified shortages.**  
If data shows we lack nurses, engineers, or seasonal farm laborers, bring people in where they’re needed. That’s a win‑win.

2. **Pair immigration with community investment.**

3. **Require employers to recruit locally first, but not indefinitely.**  
A reasonable window: advertise locally, offer fair wages, then if the jobs remain unfilled, open them to migrant workers.

4. **Ensure newcomers have a path to participate fully.**  
If someone’s allowed in to work, they should have straightforward ways to integrate—language classes, workplace protections, and clear rules.

He wasn’t anti‑immigrant; he was anti‑chaos. His guiding thought was: *“A community can be generous, but it also needs to be sustainable. Fairness is making sure newcomers succeed without current residents feeling pushed aside.”*

Policy Choices: Trade-offs Behind Four Options

Me: Finally, when it comes to actual policy choices, there are different approaches: complete openness, allowing anyone who wishes to come; admitting people based on job demand; setting numerical limits; or refusing entry to workers from other countries. J (扮演Character22_M) 回答: (as Character22_M)

I’d pick managed openness: job‑need admissions within numerical limits, with a firm humanitarian lane and a sensible family lane. No to “anyone can come,” no to “nobody can come.” We need rules that are clear, steady, and matched to what the country can absorb.

How I’d build it:

- Annual ceilings with flexibility
  Set an overall yearly ceiling, then divide it into bands: employment, family, humanitarian, and a small entrepreneurship/innovation slice. Let an independent commission recommend the numbers each year based on unemployment, wage growth at the bottom, housing vacancy rates, school capacity, and healthcare strain. Congress sets the guardrails; the commission dials within them. Publish the data so people can see the why.

- Job‑demand admissions tied to real shortages
  Fast‑track visas for verified shortage occupations—caregivers, certain trades, nurses, STEM—only if employers pay the prevailing wage, recruit locally first for a reasonable period, and contribute to training locals. For seasonal work, create portable visas (workers can switch employers) with decent housing standards and on‑site labor enforcement. Caps adjust with actual demand, not lobbying alone.

- Family reunification with focus on the nuclear family
  Prioritize spouses and minor children. Allow some extended family within the overall cap, but keep wait times and numbers realistic so we don’t promise what we can’t deliver. Family is stabilizing, but it has to fit within capacity.

- Humanitarian commitments with real support
  Keep a refugee floor we actually fund—caseworkers, housing, English classes—and speed asylum decisions to months, not years. Grant work authorization early so people can support themselves. If a claim is denied after due process, return people promptly and respectfully. If granted, help them integrate quickly.

- Integration plus enforcement so wages aren’t undercut
  One national standard: same labor laws, same protections, same consequences. Mandate E‑Verify once a legalization window has closed, and back it with serious penalties for wage theft and misclassification. Fund English, credential recognition, and civics classes so newcomers can participate fully and move up.

- An earned path for the long‑settled undocumented
  For people who’ve been here years, stayed out of serious trouble, and are working and paying in—offer a one‑time earned process: background checks, fines, taxes, English/civics, and then a line to permanent status. It’s about cleaning up the shadow system and restoring the rule of law.

- Border and ports: order with legal alternatives
  Tighten ports of entry where most contraband moves, use modern tech between ports, and pair enforcement with legal channels that actually function. Work with source countries and local communities so the pressure isn’t all at the border.

- Cost‑sharing with communities
  If the federal government admits more people, it should help cities and towns with schools, clinics, and housing. Otherwise resentment is guaranteed. Tie admissions increases to housing production and give locals a seat at the table.

Why this mix:

- Complete openness ignores capacity—housing, schools, wages—and erodes public trust.
- Blanket refusal ignores our economic needs and our values; it also fuels illegal hiring and a shadow labor market.
- Numerical limits alone are too blunt; they should flex with conditions.
- Job‑only systems miss family stability and humanitarian obligations; family‑only systems can misalign with labor needs.

Fairness, to me, is predictable rules, equal labor standards, and a pace of change communities can absorb. Belonging comes from participation and clear paths to it. Community survives when generosity is paired with order. This approach tries to hold all three together.
