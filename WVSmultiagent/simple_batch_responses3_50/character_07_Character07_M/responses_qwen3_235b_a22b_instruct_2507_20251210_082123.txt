I wouldn’t go for *complete* openness—not because I don’t believe in welcome, but because real care means responsibility. **First: Admit people based on humanitarian need *and* labor demand—but with protections.**  
Let refugees, asylum seekers, families fleeing violence come in safely and legally. Streamline the process so people aren’t stuck in limbo for years. At the same time, yes—let workers come in to fill real shortages, especially in caregiving, agriculture, construction. But tie that to strong labor laws: no exploitation, full wage protection, path to residency if they’re contributing. No more guest worker programs that trap people in debt and silence.

And here’s the key: **when we bring people in, we invest in integration.** Not assimilation—integration. Language classes. Job training. Legal support. Schools that honor bilingual kids instead of punishing them. Neighborhoods with clinics, childcare, transit—so newcomers aren’t dumped in the cracks.

**Second: Set fair numerical limits—not arbitrary caps, but data-driven numbers based on capacity.**  
How many people can our housing, schools, hospitals absorb *if properly funded*? That should be the question—not “how few can we get away with?” If we fund public services fairly, we can welcome more. Right now, limits are political, not practical. We cap visas while nursing homes beg for staff? That’s not logic. That’s neglect.

And let’s fix the backlog. My cousin’s wife is from Jamaica. They’ve been waiting *ten years* for her green card. Ten years! People shouldn’t have to live in fear because paperwork moves like molasses.

**Third: Create regional pathways—not just for high-skilled workers, but for displaced communities.**  
Like, if climate change is pushing farmers out of Central America, help rebuild there *and* offer migration options here—with dignity. Same for cities in the U.S. losing population. Why not resettle refugee families in Buffalo or Gary with housing, jobs, support networks? Revive dying towns *with* compassion.

**And always—always—a path to citizenship.**  
No permanent underclass. No “forever foreigners.” If you’re paying taxes, raising kids, following the law, after a reasonable time—you earn full membership. That’s fairness. That’s how you build belonging.

Because integration doesn’t happen by accident. It happens when policy says: *You are part of this now.* When schools teach about all our histories. When police departments reflect the communities they serve. When city councils listen to immigrant voices.

Look—I’m not naive. Resources matter. Culture shifts take time. Tensions flare. But I also know this: my liberation is bound up with yours. As a Black man in America, I didn’t get free alone. Freedom is shared—or it ain’t real.

So the policy I want? One that’s tough enough to work, but kind enough to mean something. Where borders aren’t weapons, but gateways. Where we say, “Come—and let’s build something better, together.”
