I’m not for the extremes. “Anyone can come” isn’t a plan, and “no one can come” ignores reality and our values. What that looks like to me:

- Tie work visas to real need and equal standards. Employers post jobs at or above the prevailing wage, show local recruiting and training attempts, then get visas if the shortage is real. Visas should be portable so workers can leave a bad boss. Big penalties for wage theft and misclassification, up the chain.

- Keep a steady, orderly humanitarian track. Maintain a refugee cap we can actually support, with solid vetting. Fix asylum so cases are decided in months, not years, and issue work permits quickly so people aren’t stuck idle. That’s both humane and practical.

- Prioritize close family reunification, with clear sponsorship. Spouses and minor kids first; others wait longer. Don’t upend the labor mix while people arrive who’ll plant roots and contribute.

- Create a realistic path for the long-settled. If you’ve been here for years, clean record, paying taxes, you should be able to earn legal status—fines, background checks, English/civics courses, then a line to a green card. Otherwise we just keep an underground labor market that undercuts everyone.

- Seasonal and essential sectors get tailored lanes. Agriculture, elder care, maybe certain trades: short-term visas that match the calendar, with housing standards and wage floors. Employers chip into a local training fund as the price of using these programs.

- Enforce the back door, cleanly. Mandatory, simple worker verification for all employers, with a fast fix process for database errors. Real fines for cheaters. That protects both local workers and migrants from being used.

- Fund the places doing the welcoming. If a town’s classrooms and clinic are absorbing new neighbors—local or immigrant—state/federal money should follow headcount. And we should build more housing so we’re not cramming more people into the same few rentals.

- Help people knit in. English classes, credential recognition, and basic community orientation. It pays off when nurses can practice as nurses and neighbors can talk to each other at the school gate.

Why this mix feels fair to me:
- Predictability: People and businesses can plan. No whiplash.
- Level field: Same wages and safety for everyone—no discount labor.
- Humanity with order: We meet our obligations without chaos.
- Local voice: Numbers respond to real conditions on the ground.

Trade-offs I accept:
- In a downturn, we dial work visas down even if some employers grumble. Locals need first crack.
- In true shortages—say, night-shift aides—visas go up, but only with wage floors and training funds so we’re building our own bench too.
- We won’t satisfy folks who want absolute open borders or a total shut door. I can live with that if the rules are clear and enforced.

Red lines for me:
- No system designed to hold wages down.
- No tolerance for illegal hiring or wage theft.
- No dragging asylum out for years; decide cases fast and fair.
- No dumping newcomers on towns without resources to absorb them.

Bottom line, “same rules, same lines.” Hire here when we can, bring folks in when we truly can’t, keep the standards equal, and give communities the support to make it work. That’s a policy I could explain at a town meeting and look my neighbors—old and new—in the eye.
